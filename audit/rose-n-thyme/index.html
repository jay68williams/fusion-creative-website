I

The store, as a stranger sees it

Every flower order needs two things the product page does not ask for

On the Sophia page on a phone there is no delivery date picker and no card message field. Size, vase and chocolates are there, and the price sits at 683px with the button at 1,216px. Someone sending flowers for a birthday on Thursday has to guess whether you will deliver on Thursday, and has nowhere to write the message. Every national sender asks both questions before the price. It is the single biggest conversion gap on the site.

Nationwide is the headline. Four products are in the collection.

The top bar says "We delivery flowers Nationwide" (that typo is the first line every visitor reads) and the menu has a UK-Wide Delivery item. The Nationwide Delivery collection behind it holds four products, and the shipping policy prices only Glasgow postcodes, £5 to any G postcode, with nothing about the rest of the UK. The promise that should win you the country is currently four products and a missing price.

"Your flowers arrive fresh in water, beautifully presented in our signature branded boxes."rosenthyme.co.uk homepage. This is the ad. It is not yet the offer.

Your best repeat product is hidden, and your best proof is invisible

The subscription is not in the menu and not linked from the homepage; it has one photograph where every bouquet has three. And there is no review anywhere on the store: no widget on the product pages, no Trustpilot profile (yours is unclaimed with zero reviews), while your Facebook page is at 100% recommend from 60 reviews. The proof exists. A stranger arriving from an ad cannot see it.

The words undersell the ladder

Sophia steps from £45 to £51 with the Isle of Skye bar, £65 with a vase, £82.95 with a vase and truffles, without leaving the page. That is well built. But the shelf is called "Products to Compliment" and the menu says "Complimentary Gifts", which reads as free. "Finish the gift" says what it is.

The catalogue behind the store needs a tidy before any shopping ads

Meta and Google build product ads from the catalogue data, not from what the page looks like. In yours, 72 of 85 products have no product type, the large-head hydrangeas are filed under Candy & Chocolate, there are four separate "Casket Spray" listings and five "Classic Wreath" listings, and the wreath workshops spell December "Decemeber" twice. None of it is visible to a customer. All of it is visible to an ad platform deciding who to show your bouquets to.

III

The angles we would test first

"Arrives in water" against the letterbox

Fifteen seconds: the bouquet built by hand in the shop, wrapped, dropped into the box in water, and opened at the other end looking like the picture. Then the line. It is the exact answer to the complaint in every national sender's one-star reviews, and it lands on a nationwide collection with all thirty bouquets in it, not four.

Evidence: broken stems, dry letterbox flowers and "smaller than pictured" run through Serenata, Prestige, Freddie's and Interflora reviews this month.

The subscription as the Christmas gift

Freddie's has proven the UK will buy flowers by subscription from an ad. Yours is a better product: a hand-tied bouquet from a real shop, three, six or twelve deliveries, paid once. Sold as "the present for someone who has everything", from November, with the price table fixed and the product in the menu.

Evidence: every Freddie's ad is a subscription ad; yours is unlinked, one photo, mis-priced.

Ads by occasion, landing by occasion

Birthday, thank you, sympathy, new baby, "just because". Each ad names the reason, each lands on a page that answers it, with the date picker, the card message and the vase at the point of choosing. Interflora's "little wins" campaign is the non-occasion version of the same idea and it is their biggest set.

Evidence: your four homepage bouquets all read "From £45" with no occasion attached.

The shop is the studio

The barn doors, the green panelling, the grey carrier with the ribbon, the Hyndland window display people comment on: it is a set nobody else has. Florists' hands, the ribbon pull, the bag going out of the door. For Glasgow, run it in a ten-mile radius where no other florist is advertising at all; for the rest of the UK, it is the proof that a real person made this.

Evidence: one active florist ad in Glasgow on 23 September. Your Facebook video of the display went up this week.

Wreath workshops and the Christmas run

Three dates at £85 in the catalogue, plus thirty autumn and Halloween homeware pieces added in the last month. Local ads fill the workshops weeks before the dates; the same footage becomes the Christmas bouquet and wreath ads. Christmas, Valentine's and Mother's Day are where a florist's year is made, and each one needs its creative built the month before.

Evidence: workshops dated 29 Nov, 3 Dec and 10 Dec; homeware added 30 Aug and 5 Sep.

What we would do, in order

Fix the leaks, then fill the funnel

This week, no budget

The free fixes

Subscription prices. Delivery date and card message on every bouquet. Reviews on the product page (import the Facebook ones). Top bar typo, "Compliment" wording, subscription in the menu, nationwide delivery priced and the collection filled. Catalogue types and duplicates.

Week two

A shoot day in the shop

Hands, ribbon, box, water, door. Florists on camera if they are willing, the display if not. One day gives a month of ads and organic posts across Instagram, Facebook and a TikTok you do not have yet.

Week three

Ads live, two campaigns

Glasgow radius: the shop and the workshops. Nationwide: "arrives in water" by occasion, landing on the full bouquet range. Retargeting on the pixel that is already installed.

November onward

The subscription, then the peaks

Subscription as the Christmas gift, then Valentine's and Mother's Day creative built a month ahead each time, with the numbers from the first campaigns deciding the budget.
